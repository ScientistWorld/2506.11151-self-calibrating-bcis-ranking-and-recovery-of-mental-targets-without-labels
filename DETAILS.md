# Overview

This reproduction implements CURSOR from “Self-Calibrating BCIs: Ranking and
Recovery of Mental Targets Without Labels.”

- **Paper ID:** 2506.11151
- **Title:** Self-Calibrating BCIs: Ranking and Recovery of Mental Targets
  Without Labels
- **Domain:** Brain-computer interfaces / EEG target recovery
- **TL;DR:** The paper ranks possible mental targets from unlabeled
  EEG-stimulus interactions by testing which candidate makes the observed brain
  responses internally predictive of candidate-specific distances.

## Short Summary

The benchmark asks a solver to rank 60 candidate face latents for a hidden
mental target using only one session's paired stimulus latents and real EEG
responses. Sessions are generated by the evaluator. Stimuli and candidates are
drawn independently of the target, so only the EEG/stimulus pairing carries the
answer. Solvers are registered commands that the evaluator runs on visible
train sessions and on hidden test sessions.

## Key Results (hidden test, 48 sessions per experiment)

| Experiment | CURSOR-LR rank / top-rank distance | S-LR | Dummy (chance) |
|---|---|---|---|
| raw EEG, N = 3000 | 2.69 / 3.89 | 31.2 / 22.9 | 30.5 / 22.5 |
| EEGNet, N = 3000 | 2.52 / 6.06 | 29.4 / 23.6 | 30.5 / 22.5 |
| raw EEG, N = 1000 | 14.94 / 15.39 | 29.9 / 21.0 | 30.5 / 22.8 |

On the paper's own simulation protocol at full scale (N = 9234), CURSOR-LR
ranks the target first in all 34 runs. A no-EEG stimulus-centroid ranking
nearly matches it (rank 1.03), which is why the scored sessions use a
target-independent geometry (see `HARDENING.md`).

# Problem Definition

The task is to recover a continuous mental target from an interaction session
containing paired visual stimuli and EEG responses, without calibration labels.
Each visual stimulus is a face latent vector (512-D, from a face generator) and
each response is an EEG feature vector recorded while that face was shown.
During a session the participant holds one target face in mind; the solver
receives only the stimulus vectors, the paired EEG responses, and a set of 60
candidate face latents, exactly one of which is the participant's target.

## Research Question

Can a system rank candidate face targets so that candidates closer to the
participant's intended mental target receive better scores, using only the
unlabeled interaction data of that session?

## Why It Matters

A successful solution removes the labeled calibration phase of
brain-computer interfaces that must infer user intent over continuous target
spaces (here, any face in a generator's latent space).

## What Makes It Hard

- No labels: the solver is never told which stimuli resembled the target, nor
  the target's identity, and no pretrained EEG-to-distance decoder may be used.
- The stimuli and the candidates are drawn independently of the target, so the
  stimulus/candidate geometry by itself says nothing about which candidate is
  the target; the information is only in how the EEG responses co-vary with
  the stimuli.
- EEG responses are noisy single trials; how strongly they reflect the
  perceived similarity to the target is unknown and session-specific (EEG
  features arrive in a session-specific basis).
- Sessions can be short: one experiment has only 1000 stimulus/response pairs.

## Success Criteria

A good solver ranks the target and near-target candidates above distant ones,
does clearly better than chance and than broken controls, and gets its signal
from the pairing between EEG responses and stimuli rather than from any other
source.

# Paper's Method

CURSOR (Consistency-based Unsupervised Regression for Similarity-based
Optimization and Ranking) scores a hypothetical mental target by constructing
labels internally from that hypothesis. For a candidate latent `h`, each
observed stimulus `z_i` is mapped to a hypothetical distance `||h - z_i||_2`
(standardised). A regressor is trained to predict these distances from the
paired EEG features, and the same task is repeated after deliberately
misaligning EEG responses and stimuli (a fixed random permutation).

The score `S(h)` is the held-out shuffled/aligned RMSE ratio averaged over 10
random 90/10 ShuffleSplit folds (aligned and shuffled errors are computed on
the identical held-out fold). If `h` is the participant's target, aligned EEG
predicts the hypothesis distances better than shuffled EEG and `S(h)` is
larger. Candidates are ranked by `S(h)`; no target labels and no pretrained
EEG decoder are used.

## Implementation in this gym

- `method/cursor.py`: `cursor_score` is the scalar scikit-learn version
  (`StandardScaler` + `LinearRegression` per candidate); `cursor_scores_fast`
  runs the identical procedure for all 60 candidates at once (shared folds,
  normal equations). They agree to 7e-16 (`redteam/hardening/equivalence.json`).
  OLS with an intercept is invariant to the evaluator's per-session EEG basis.
- `method/score_sessions.py`: the solver (`--estimator lr|s_lr|dummy`).
  `s_lr` (shuffled pairs) and `dummy` (mean predictor) are the paper's
  control conditions; `baseline/run_baseline.py` registers them.
- `method/register_solvers.py`: writes `outputs/solvers/{lr,s_lr,dummy}.json`.
- `method/paper_protocol_check.py`: runs CURSOR on the paper's own
  simulation protocol (target-centred stimuli, uniform-distance candidates,
  N = 9234) as a reproduction diagnostic; not scored.

## Key Contribution

A self-calibrating score for continuous mental-target recovery: the method
asks which candidate makes the unlabeled EEG responses most internally
predictive of candidate-specific stimulus distances.

## Main Claims

- CURSOR ranks hypotheses by similarity to the unknown mental target without
  calibration labels (Table 1: LR R = -0.77, rank 6.63/60, top-rank distance
  2.90 at N = 9234).
- Linear regression outperforms the shuffled (S-LR) and Dummy controls, so the
  signal comes from EEG/stimulus alignment.
- The result holds for EEGNet embeddings (Appendix Table 2) and degrades as the
  number of observations shrinks (Figure 3).

# Evaluation

The evaluator owns every input of the comparison. It generates the sessions,
runs every registered solver on the same sessions, reads back only the raw
candidate scores, and recomputes every metric itself.

## Sessions

Each session contains:

- `stimuli`: N face latents (N x 512, float32);
- `eeg`: the EEG response recorded for each stimulus (N x p, row i belongs
  to stimulus i);
- `candidates`: 60 face latents (60 x 512), exactly one of which is the hidden
  target.

How sessions are built:

- **Geometry.** Stimuli and candidates are drawn from one target-independent
  distribution: uniform on a 2-sphere of radius 17.3 lying in a random 3-D
  affine subspace of the 512-D latent space. Every point on that sphere is
  equivalent, so geometry alone carries no information about the target. The
  target is a uniformly random candidate. The expected candidate-target
  distance is 23.1, the same as the chance level of the paper's ranking task.
- **EEG responses.** Each EEG row is a real recorded response. The released
  data contain responses recorded at 60 known stimulus-target distances
  between 0 and 46.2. Each stimulus receives an unused row recorded at the
  distance nearest to its own distance from the hidden target.
- **Session-specific EEG basis.** Within each session the EEG features are
  z-scored and multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix. Feature identity
  (channel, time window, embedding unit) is therefore not preserved, but
  rotation-invariant structure is.
- **Control sessions.** Mixed into every split are control sessions whose EEG
  rows are permuted across stimuli. The solver is not told which sessions are
  controls.

Each split holds 48 scored sessions and 16 control sessions per experiment. The
train split (`eval/train/`) is visible and can be exported with its answers:

```
python eval/train/evaluate_cursor.py --export-sessions DIR
```

The hidden test (`eval/test/`) uses the same generator with a hidden seed.

## Experiments

| Experiment | EEG representation | N per session | Weight |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| `unbiased_ranking_raw_eeg` | windowed raw EEG, 29 channels x 7 windows = 203 features | 3000 | 0.50 |
| `unbiased_ranking_eegnet` | released 176-D EEGNet embedding, same sessions as above | 3000 | 0.25 |
| `unbiased_ranking_raw_eeg_low_data` | windowed raw EEG, independent sessions | 1000 | 0.25 |

## Solver interface

A solver is registered by a manifest `outputs/solvers/<name>.json` of the form
`{"command": ["python", "my_solver.py", "--sessions", "{sessions}", "--output", "{output}"]}`.
The command runs from the workspace root. `{sessions}` is a directory holding
`index.json` (session ids and the EEG representation) and one `<id>.npz` per
session; `data/cursor_data.py:read_session_dir` reads it. The solver writes
`{"scores": {"<session id>": [60 floats]}}` to `{output}`. A higher score means
the candidate is more likely to be the target.

`scripts/evaluate.sh` / `scripts/evaluate_train.sh` score every registered
solver on the train sessions (`scoring/scores.json`, `scoring/scores_train.json`).
`scripts/evaluate_test.sh` scores them on the hidden test
(`scoring/scores_test.json`).

## Metrics (per experiment, mean over the 48 scored sessions)

Ties among scores are resolved as the expectation over uniformly random
tie-breaking.

- `top_rank_distance` (primary, lower is better): latent distance from the
  top-scored candidate to the target.
- `target_rank` (lower): rank of the target among the 60 candidates.
- `score_distance_pearson_r` (lower): Pearson correlation between scores and
  the candidates' distances to the target; constant scores count as 0.
- `target_top1_accuracy`, `target_top5_accuracy`, `reciprocal_target_rank`
  (higher).
- `near_target_top_rank_rate` (higher): fraction of sessions whose top
  candidate is within distance 5 of the target.

Random guessing gives rank 30.5, top-rank distance about 23, r = 0, and top-1
accuracy 1/60.

## Gates (a failed gate scores the solver as failed on that experiment)

These outcomes count as a failure:

- the command fails or times out;
- the output is unreadable;
- any session is missing;
- any session has the wrong number of scores;
- any score is non-finite.

The control-session gate also applies. On control sessions every solver is at
chance in expectation (mean rank 30.5). A solver whose mean control-session rank
is below 30.5 − 4 standard errors (13.2) gets its ranking from something other
than the EEG/stimulus pairing, and fails.

A failed solver receives the worst attainable value of every metric: r = +1,
rank 60, maximal distance, and zero accuracies.
